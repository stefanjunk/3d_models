# Food-contact ceramic serving ware

A decorative ceramic bowl is not proven food-safe merely because the clay or glaze is sold as “food safe.” Compliance and safe use concern the final fired article under its intended food, time, temperature, cleaning, and damage conditions.

## Scope

This guidance is design-oriented, not legal certification. For products placed on the market, verify the law and test standards current in the target country at the time of production. In the EU, ceramic food-contact articles are regulated under the general food-contact framework and ceramic-specific rules; the European Commission was actively revising ceramic and vitreous food-contact limits in 2026. Germany also implements ceramic migration requirements through national consumer-goods rules.

## What can migrate

Ceramic bodies, glazes, stains, overglaze decorations, metallic lusters, decals, and contaminants can release substances depending on composition, firing, glaze quality, food acidity, temperature, contact time, wear, and defects. Lead and cadmium are traditional regulated concerns, but current risk assessment can include additional metals.

Do not rely on appearance. A glossy surface can still be improperly matured or chemically unstable.

## Design rules for the serving bowl

### Food-contact interior

Prefer:

- a smooth continuous interior;
- generous radii at the bottom and wall transition;
- no deep pinholes, crazing, crawling, shivering, sharp specks, or unglazed absorbent patches;
- a stable rim that can be inspected and cleaned;
- decoration under a qualified glaze or outside the food-contact region unless the full system is validated;
- a geometry that drains completely in the dishwasher and on the rack.

Avoid:

- needle-fine engraving inside the bowl;
- deep grooves that retain fruit juice, oil, detergent, or soil;
- sharp raised points that chip;
- a foot ring or double wall that traps dirty water;
- hidden hollow chambers without open drainage;
- texture where cutlery will abrade the surface.

For highly ornamented ware, put the finest relief on the **exterior** and keep the interior simpler. Broad shallow interior decoration can be tested, but design the glaze thickness and firing so valleys do not become pinholes or uncleanable crevices.

### Rim and foot

- Round the drinking/handling edge and avoid a mold seam on the lip.
- Give the foot enough area for firing stability.
- Keep the unglazed foot outside food contact and smooth it after firing.
- Add drain interruptions to a broad foot ring if it would otherwise trap dishwasher water.

## Mold route

For porcelain/stoneware slip casting:

1. Create the compensated positive bowl master or negative case.
2. Split the working plaster mold so no section locks below the rim or exterior relief.
3. Use a removable top pour spout/reservoir.
4. Keep the plaster casting face porous and clean.
5. Fill conditioned slip, dwell to target wall, drain completely, and allow the body to stiffen/shrink away.
6. Open sections in the defined order and support the fragile rim.
7. Fettle seams without thinning the wall.
8. Dry evenly, bisque, glaze, fire, and inspect.

A bowl with a flared rim and no exterior undercut may use two side sections plus a base/foot section. Deep exterior ornament can require three or four side sections or a redesigned relief.

## Glaze fit and dishwasher durability

“Dishwasher-safe” is separate from migration compliance. Repeated hot alkaline washing and thermal cycling can reveal:

- crazing or delayed crazing;
- glaze/body mismatch;
- cutlery marking and abrasion;
- fading or attack of decoration;
- water entry through exposed porous areas;
- chipping at relief and rim;
- trapped water in feet or double walls.

Qualify the exact body–glaze–decoration–firing combination. Include accelerated dishwasher cycles, thermal-shock/temperature-cycle tests appropriate to the product, stain/cleanability checks, and post-test inspection. Repeat migration testing when process changes could affect release.

## Production validation plan

For a commercial or formal food-safe claim:

1. Lock exact body, glaze, stains/decals, firing schedule, kiln loading, and supplier lots.
2. Use representative worst-case articles, including maximum decoration and difficult kiln positions.
3. Inspect for glaze defects and dimensional issues.
4. Commission migration testing through a competent/accredited laboratory using current applicable methods and limits for the market.
5. Document traceability, declarations, test reports, batch controls, and change management.
6. Revalidate after changes to body, glaze, pigment, firing, thickness, supplier, or decoration.

Do not infer legal compliance from a home vinegar/lemon test. Such tests may reveal obvious instability but are not a substitute for standardized migration testing.

## Hygiene-oriented ornament limits

Use a transfer coupon with the final glaze. Test a matrix such as:

| Groove width | Depth | Location |
|---:|---:|---|
| 0.5 mm | 0.2 mm | exterior only |
| 0.8 mm | 0.3 mm | exterior and interior test |
| 1.2 mm | 0.4 mm | interior test |
| 2.0 mm | 0.6 mm | interior test |

After firing and dishwasher cycling, inspect under magnification and with a soil-cleaning protocol. Select geometry from measured cleanability and glaze behavior rather than aesthetic CAD alone.

## User-facing claims

Only use claims that the evidence supports. Distinguish:

- decorative only;
- dry snacks/whole fruit only;
- food-contact qualified for stated conditions;
- dishwasher tested under stated cycle count;
- microwave/oven/freezer suitability, each separately qualified.

A mold-design skill should never silently upgrade one claim into another.
